I'm not a generalist and I'm not a specialist. I'm a systems thinker who sits at the intersection of business, technology, and human behavior.

Who I am

The operating model behind the work

I've spent my career asking one question: "Why does this process exist, and how could it work better?" That question has taken me from digital marketing into CRM systems, from CRM into workflow automation, and from automation into enterprise innovation operations.

My background sits at the crossroads of business operations and technology implementation. I'm comfortable in boardrooms and in system configuration screens. I understand both the business problem and the technical constraint — and I design solutions that satisfy both.

I'm drawn to problems where the gap between how something should work and how it actually works is largest. That gap is where systems thinking creates the most value.

Working Style

How I work

Structured

I approach ambiguous problems by first building a mental model of the system — mapping inputs, outputs, dependencies, and failure points before designing solutions.

Cross-functional

I operate fluently across business, technology, and people dimensions. I translate between technical constraints and business needs in both directions.

Systems-first

I solve problems by building systems, not one-off fixes. Every solution is designed to handle the next 10 cases, not just the current one.

Practical

I bias toward what can be implemented and adopted over what's theoretically optimal. Execution without adoption is just a prototype.

Fast learner

I've built systems across domains I didn't start in — from innovation management to Outlook add-ins. I learn the domain, find the system, and build the solution.

Long-term thinker

I design systems with extensibility in mind. The systems I build today should support the use cases I haven't imagined yet.

Operating Principles

What I believe about systems

These aren't rules I follow — they're truths I've learned by watching systems succeed and fail in real organizations.

01

Simplicity scales

Complexity is the enemy of adoption. Simple systems that everyone uses beat sophisticated systems that no one understands.

02

Adoption matters more than features

The measure of a system is not its capability — it's the percentage of the intended audience that actually uses it consistently.

03

AI should reduce friction

The best AI implementations are invisible — they eliminate the friction in a workflow so seamlessly that users don't realize AI is involved.

04

Systems should fit humans, not vice versa

Forcing people to adapt to bad systems creates workarounds, shadow processes, and broken data. Design systems around real human behavior.

05

Every inefficiency is a system waiting to be designed

When I see manual work, I see an automation opportunity. When I see inconsistency, I see a process that needs structure. When I see data loss, I see a system gap.
